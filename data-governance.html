Focus Area 03 · Research Topic 01

Data Governance & Digital Trust

Explore how clear responsibilities, reliable data practices and rights-respecting institutions can make digital services more trustworthy.

Overview

Data governance is the set of roles, rules and processes that shape how data is collected, described, protected, accessed, shared and retained. Good governance makes accountability visible: people know who is responsible for a dataset, how its quality is assessed, which uses are permitted and how errors or complaints are handled.

Digital trust grows when institutions use data transparently, keep it appropriately secure, explain consequential decisions and respect the people represented in their records. It is not created by technology alone; it depends on lawful purpose, competent stewardship, meaningful oversight and dependable remedies.

Key research areas

  • Data ownership, stewardship and accountability across an organisation.
  • Quality, provenance, integrity, classification and lifecycle management.
  • Access controls and responsible sharing between public bodies and partners.
  • Transparency, notice, consent where applicable, and public participation.
  • Governance models that support interoperability without weakening safeguards.

Challenges and risks

Unclear ownership can leave inaccurate records uncorrected and make oversight difficult. Inconsistent formats and weak provenance can undermine decisions. Excessive collection, overbroad access, secondary use without proper safeguards and insecure exchanges can harm individuals and erode confidence. Organisations must also manage competing needs: open and useful services, confidentiality, privacy, security and reliable retention practices.

Legal and regulatory dimensions

Governance should be designed around the laws that apply to the data and the institution. In India, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is relevant to processing digital personal data; the Information Technology Act, 2000 and applicable rules may also be relevant. Sectoral requirements and contractual duties can add further controls. The specific obligations depend on context and should be verified with qualified counsel.

Practical applications

Public agencies can assign accountable data stewards, publish understandable data-use notices, document access decisions and establish correction and escalation routes. Businesses can map data flows, review vendor access and use quality checks before data informs services. Citizens benefit when institutions explain why information is needed and provide usable routes to exercise applicable rights.

Research questions

  • Which institutional arrangements make data stewardship accountable across departments?
  • How can public data-sharing improve services while protecting privacy and security?
  • What evidence helps people assess the trustworthiness of automated, data-driven services?
  • How should organisations measure data quality and remedy consequential errors?
