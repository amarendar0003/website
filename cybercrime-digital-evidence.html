Cyber Security · Research Topic 04

Cybercrime & Digital Evidence

Examine investigation practices that preserve the integrity, context and lawful handling of electronic evidence.

Overview

Cybercrime investigations may involve evidence from computers, mobile devices, networks, cloud services and online accounts. Digital evidence is fragile and contextual: it can be altered by routine system activity, distributed across providers or difficult to interpret without an understanding of how it was collected. Careful methods help investigators and institutions preserve relevant material while respecting legal authority and individual rights.

Digital forensics applies documented procedures to acquire, examine and report on digital material. Sound practice requires trained personnel, suitable tools, reproducible methods and clear separation between observed facts and interpretation.

Research and training focus

  • Cybercrime investigation workflows and inter-agency coordination.
  • Forensic acquisition, examination, analysis and transparent reporting.
  • Evidence preservation, provenance, documentation and chain of custody.
  • Integrity checks, validated tools and reproducibility of examination steps.
  • Training on safe handling, incident referrals and rights-respecting practice.

Evidence and investigation challenges

Volatile data can disappear quickly, while actions taken during collection can change a device or record. Evidence may span multiple jurisdictions, service providers and time zones. Attribution is technically and legally complex; an IP address or account association alone does not necessarily identify a person. Investigators must consider alternative explanations, document limitations and protect unrelated or sensitive information.

Legal and ethical considerations

Collection and examination should be grounded in proper authority, a defined scope and safeguards for privacy and confidentiality. Indian matters may engage the Information Technology Act, 2000 and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, among other applicable laws and procedural requirements. Whether particular material is admissible depends on the governing law, its acquisition and the facts. This overview is not legal advice.

Practical applications

Institutions can establish evidence-preservation procedures, escalation routes and referral criteria before an incident occurs. Investigators can document custody transfers, acquisition conditions, tool versions and analytical steps. Training can help staff recognise potential evidence, avoid unnecessary handling and coordinate early with authorised forensic specialists and legal advisers.

Research questions

  • Which practices improve reproducibility and transparency in digital forensic reporting?
  • How can institutions preserve relevant evidence while minimising collection of unrelated data?
  • What procedures support cross-organisational coordination while maintaining clear accountability?
  • How should training address the limits of attribution and automated forensic tools?
