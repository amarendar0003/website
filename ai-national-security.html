Focus Area 03 · Research Topic 04

AI & National Security

Consider how AI can support public purposes while keeping security, human judgement, accountability and fundamental rights in view.

Overview

Artificial intelligence can assist analysis, service delivery and security operations, but the quality and consequences of these uses depend on their design, data, deployment context and oversight. National-security settings can involve sensitive information and high-impact decisions, making it important to assess not only model performance but also authority, proportionality, auditability and routes for review.

AI-related security concerns also run in both directions: systems may help identify threats, while adversaries may use automation, synthetic media or model weaknesses. Responsible governance therefore links technical testing with institutional accountability and clear limits on use.

Key research areas

  • Accountability, documentation, explainability and meaningful human oversight.
  • AI-assisted cybersecurity, threat analysis and operational decision support.
  • Adversarial manipulation, data poisoning, model misuse and system security.
  • Synthetic media, deepfakes, misinformation and methods for responsible verification.
  • Public-sector procurement, evaluation, monitoring and routes to challenge decisions.

Challenges and risks

Training data may be incomplete, biased or unsuitable for a new purpose. Opaque systems can make it difficult to explain errors or assign responsibility. Automation bias may cause staff to over-rely on recommendations, while false positives can consume resources or affect people unfairly. Models and datasets can also be attacked, leak sensitive information or change in performance as operating conditions shift.

Legal and regulatory dimensions

AI deployments remain subject to applicable privacy, cybersecurity, administrative and sectoral law; AI is not a legal exemption. In India, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and Information Technology Act, 2000 may be relevant depending on the data and system. Governance should also consider procurement terms, security standards and applicable official guidance. Specific obligations must be determined for each deployment rather than inferred from a general AI label.

Practical applications

Public institutions can document intended uses, assess impacts before deployment, test systems against representative conditions and define human review and escalation. Security teams can treat models and data pipelines as part of the threat surface. Communication teams can establish verification and correction procedures for manipulated media without treating automated detection as conclusive evidence.

Research questions

  • What oversight is effective when AI informs consequential public decisions?
  • How should public agencies evaluate model security and performance over time?
  • Which institutional procedures best address synthetic media without undermining expression?
  • How can procurement allocate accountability across agencies, vendors and system operators?
